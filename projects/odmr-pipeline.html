Turning 10,000 noisy sweeps into a working diamond magnetic-field sensor

I built the Python pipeline behind a diamond-based magnetic-field sensor. It drives the instruments, captures 10,000 sweeps, rejects glitched ones and fits every spectrum: up to 8 resonance lines, R² ≥ 0.97 in all 10 conditions. Readings ran below the coil's predicted field; one constant 0.61 mT offset, fitted afterward and consistent with stray magnetization, brings 0.4 to 0.8 A within about 1.1σ of theory.

MotivationWhy it mattered

NV centers, atom-sized defects in diamond, glow red under a green laser and dim by a few percent at a microwave resonance near 2.9 GHz. A magnetic field splits that resonance, and the split gives the field (optically detected magnetic resonance, ODMR).

Objective: build this sensor from teaching-lab hardware and test it against coil theory: clean without distorting, detect the dip, resolve the splitting, match theory. One sweep can't show the dip (Figure 3).

What I learned

Build tools that make problems visible. My first program showed no convincing resonance (Figure 6); kept-vs-rejected plots and a read-only instrument check found most fixes. And a gap with theory is a result to explain: replacing a “sample flush with the coil” shortcut with the full coil geometry cut the gap from about 30σ to 3 to 13σ.

Technical detailsHow it works

Methods in depth

Acquisition

A function generator drives a slow sawtooth into the microwave source's tuning input, sweeping about 0.34 GHz across the resonance. The generator's real output did not match its display, so calibrations use scope-measured voltages. A read-only diagnostic script showed the scope's trigger had been left in free-running AUTO mode, with its level only about 25 mV above the ramp's noisy minimum. It now triggers on the sawtooth's rising edge at a level 15% of the ramp's height above its measured minimum, in NORMAL sweep mode, and records about 10,000 samples per channel. We calibrated voltage to frequency with a frequency counter, averaging three slow-motion-video readings per sweep endpoint (2.6645 and 3.0020 GHz). The code reuses the intercept from an earlier calibration that the notebook marks as inaccurate, which puts plotted frequencies roughly 50 MHz above the counter readings. That shifts every line equally, so line spacings and fields are unaffected.

Cleaning

A real resonance cannot cause a fast jump: its steepest slope works out to about 0.06 mV per sample, far below the detector noise, so any threshold above the noise floor is safe. The textbook median/MAD sigma-clipping rule collapsed to zero because the signal is heavily quantized (59% of sample-to-sample jumps are exactly zero). A 99.5th-percentile threshold rejected about 95% of sweeps, because each sweep's worst jump is a maximum over roughly 9,700 samples. On an earlier capture the 99.9th percentile reproduced my hand-tuned split exactly (847 of 1,000 kept) and the same −7.78% dip, so I shipped it as an option and documented both failed attempts in the code. Surviving sweeps get a 5-sample rolling-median despike. Figure 3's gray trace is the capture's first sweep, with only the spike filter applied.

Frequency axis and averaging

At high record lengths, each sample's true voltage step is smaller than the channel noise, so sorting raw samples by frequency scrambled their order and made a jagged spectrum. Fitting each sweep's ramp with a straight line restored a smooth, monotonic axis. Sweeps are then interpolated onto a common 1,000-point grid, averaged, and reported as percent below the off-resonance baseline, with the standard error propagated into the contrast.

Adaptive fit

Each spectrum is fit to a sum of N Lorentzian dips (SciPy curve_fit), with N set by peak-finding on the data and capped at 8 (4 crystal axes × 2 spin states). Every line gets its own depth; the linewidth is shared. Two fixed two-line fits failed first: seeded on the deepest dips, the fit locked onto the least field-sensitive pair and drifted instead of widening; seeded on the outer dips, it collapsed into two needle-thin lines sitting on just two data points. A shared-depth version then missed 0.4 A's shoulders (about 1% deep; R² 0.963, vs. 0.993 with per-line depth).

Field and uncertainty

The outermost pair's spacing S gives the field through S/2 = √(E² + (γB)²), with γ = 28.025 GHz/T and the strain term E taken from the 0 A fit. Readings span 0 to 3.1 mT, up to about 60× Earth's field. Theory uses the exact finite-solenoid field at the sample's 0.75 cm standoff beyond the coil's end. An earlier version assumed the sample sat flush with the coil (and used slightly different coil dimensions), which had left a discrepancy of about 30σ. Each measured field carries its fit uncertainty (from the splitting and E). The theory band combines the supply's datasheet accuracy and ruler readings (±0.07 cm). At 0.5 A the predicted field is 2.14 ± 0.14 mT, and the standoff term (0.13 mT) dominates that budget. At 0.7 A the measured field is 2.365 ± 0.003 mT. That ± is fit precision only: the voltage-to-frequency slope's own uncertainty, which would scale every measured field, is not yet propagated, though the repo documents a method for it. The fitted 0.61 mT offset is too large to be Earth's field. At 0.1 A the offset model expects a net field of about −0.18 mT, which readings cannot show because of the strain floor.

What it took to get a clean spectrum

Getting a clean spectrum took fixes on both sides: detector gain and a sign bug to see the dip at all, then locking the trigger and unscrambling the frequency axis to make it sharp. The same habit fixed the fits: when a model collapses, count what the data shows before adding complexity. The gap with theory that remains (3 to 13σ) is consistent with stray magnetization near the sample, which I have not tested yet.

What I started from

My acquisition and analysis scripts follow the design of a LabVIEW program in the lab handout, and the fit extends the handout's two- and four-dip models, which give every line the same depth. What I added on top: the data-derived glitch threshold, the per-sweep frequency-axis fit, the adaptive N-line fit with per-line depths, and the uncertainty propagation.
